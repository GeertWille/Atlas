The village that bought its own substation

When the grid operator put a rural substation up for sale, 640 households in the Harrow valley formed a company in eleven days. They have not had an unplanned outage since.

TE
Tomas Ebner in Harrow Cross
4 November 2026 · 5 min read

The substation at Harrow Cross is a fenced square of gravel the size of two tennis courts, with three grey transformers and a hut. For forty years nobody in the valley gave it a thought. Then, in the spring of last year, a notice appeared on the gate: the operator intended to dispose of the asset.

What the notice meant, in practice, was that the valley's connection to the wider grid would be owned by whoever bid highest, and that the bidder most often mentioned was a data-centre developer from two counties away.

"We were not against data centres," says Aoife Brannagh, who chairs the co-operative that now owns the site. "We were against finding out in five years that our own line was full."

Eleven days

The sale had a short window. The co-operative was registered on a Monday, opened a share offer on the Wednesday and closed it the Friday of the following week with pledges from 640 of the valley's 700 households. The smallest was forty pounds. The largest came from the dairy, which uses more electricity than the rest of the valley combined.

They paid a little over the guide price. The operator, by its own account, was surprised that anybody local had read the notice.

Owning a substation turns out to be mostly paperwork. The equipment is maintained under contract by the same engineers as before. What changed is who decides what gets connected, and in what order.

The first decision was to say yes to things the old owner had kept in a queue: a solar array on the dairy's roofs, a battery behind the village hall, and chargers at the bus turning circle. The second was to publish, every month, how much room was left.

What ownership buys

The harbour town of Port Calder made a similar bet on a different asset, as we reported in our account of its tidal turbines and the night tariff built around them. In both places the hardware mattered less than who got to set the rules for using it.

Brannagh is careful not to oversell the result. Bills in the valley are lower, by about a tenth, mostly because the dairy's solar is used next door instead of being exported at a poor price. The absence of outages owes something to a mild winter.

But the queue is gone. A farmer who wants to put panels on a barn gets an answer at the next monthly meeting, from neighbours who will also have to live with it.

Regulators are watching. A community that owns the wires it depends on is unusual enough that the rulebook has little to say about it, and two larger villages have already asked the co-operative for its paperwork.

"Take the forms," Brannagh tells them. "The forms were the easy part. The hard part was getting 640 people to answer the door."

The data-centre developer, for what it is worth, found a site nearer the motorway. Its connection is expected in 2031.
