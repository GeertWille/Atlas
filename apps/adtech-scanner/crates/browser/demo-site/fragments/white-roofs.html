What a white roof is worth in August

One city painted nine hundred flat roofs white and measured what happened underneath. The answer is less than the brochures promised and more than the sceptics expected.

CR
Carmen Ruiz in Valdemora
30 October 2026 · 5 min read

On the hottest afternoon of last summer, a thermometer taped under the roof of a clothing workshop in the east of Valdemora read 41 degrees. The same instrument, in the same spot, one year and one coat of paint later, read 35.

Six degrees is the kind of number that ends up on a poster. The city's own engineers would like people to read the rest of the table first.

Valdemora has painted nine hundred flat roofs white since 2023, most of them on workshops, schools and the low apartment blocks that ring the old centre. It also did something cities rarely do: it left three hundred comparable roofs alone and measured both groups.

Where the gain is

The top floor is where a white roof earns its keep. Rooms directly under a painted roof were on average 3.1 degrees cooler at the afternoon peak. One floor down, the difference was under a degree. On the ground floor it could not be distinguished from zero.

That matters for who benefits. In Valdemora the top floors of older blocks are where rents are lowest and air conditioning rarest.

The effect on the street is smaller still. Measured at head height, the air in painted districts was a quarter of a degree cooler, within the range the engineers call noise.

"A white roof is not shade," says Irene Montalvo, who ran the study. "It keeps heat out of the building. It does very little for the person waiting at the bus stop."

The winter bill

There is also a cost that the posters leave out. A roof that reflects the sun in August reflects it in January too, and heating use in the top-floor flats rose by about four per cent. In a city with Valdemora's winters that is a good trade. Two hundred kilometres north it would not be.

Paint is not forever either. After three summers the oldest roofs had lost a third of their reflectivity to dust and soot. The city now budgets for a wash every second spring.

For a sense of scale the engineers compare the programme with a very different local measure, the half-hourly night tariff described in our report from Port Calder: both work by moving a load in time or space rather than by generating anything.

Montalvo's summary fits on one line of her report. For top-floor rooms without cooling, white paint is the cheapest degree the city can buy. For everything else, plant a tree.

The workshop in the east has drawn its own conclusion. This year the owner painted the roof of the storeroom as well, and moved the cutting tables upstairs.
