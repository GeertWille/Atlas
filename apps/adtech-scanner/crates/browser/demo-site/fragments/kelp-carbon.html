Kelp farmers want a seat at the carbon table

Seaweed grows faster than any forest and sinks when it dies. The people who farm it say that should count for something. The people who count carbon are not yet sure what.

LH
Laila Haddad in Skerry Sound
2 November 2026 · 5 min read

From the deck of the workboat the farm is nearly invisible: a grid of orange buoys, each the size of a football, holding up four kilometres of rope. Everything that matters hangs below. By April the lines carry curtains of sugar kelp three metres long, grown from threads no thicker than a hair in five months.

Ruairi Macrae has farmed here for six seasons. He sells most of the harvest to a company that makes food thickeners and the rest to a brewer. What he would like to sell, and cannot, is the carbon.

"A forest gets a credit for standing there," he says. "My kelp pulls down more per hectare than a spruce plantation and I get paid for the soup."

The trouble with counting water

The difficulty is not whether kelp absorbs carbon. It plainly does. The difficulty is what happens next. A tree keeps its carbon in wood for decades. Kelp is harvested and eaten, or it breaks off and drifts, and only the part that sinks into deep sediment is out of circulation for long.

How large that part is depends on currents, depth and appetite, since a great many animals eat seaweed on its way down. Estimates for farms like this one range from a twentieth of the crop to a quarter.

Carbon registries have been burned before by generous assumptions, and they are in no hurry. A draft method published this autumn would credit only kelp that is deliberately sunk in water deeper than a thousand metres, which no farm on this coast can reach.

The farmers' answer is that they are being held to a standard no forest meets. Their trade body wants credit for the measured share, however small, and for something registries do not count at all: a harvest that replaces a product made from oil.

A smaller, surer claim

There is a middle path, and it looks like the one taken by communities that stopped waiting for a national scheme. In the Harrow valley, a village that bought its own substation now decides locally what connects to it. Macrae's co-operative is trying the marine equivalent: selling verified tonnes directly to two local firms, with the sampling paid for by the buyers.

The numbers are modest. Last season the farm could document nine tonnes of carbon in sediment under its lines, about what forty cars emit in a year.

"Nine honest tonnes," Macrae says, coiling a line. "I would rather sell nine honest ones than nine hundred that somebody takes back."

Whether the registries follow is an open question. The scientists doing the sampling say five more seasons of data would settle most of the argument.

The kelp, meanwhile, does not wait for a method. The next crop goes on the lines in the first week of November.
