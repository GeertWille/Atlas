The tide-powered town that sells its nights

A harbour of 9,000 people turned a failing ferry dock into the cheapest overnight electricity on the coast. The engineers say the hard part was never the water.

IO
Ines Okafor in Port Calder
9 November 2026 · 9 min read

At twenty past two in the morning, when the tide in the Calder narrows is running hardest, the bakery on Quay Street switches its ovens on. Nobody is there to do it. A relay in the back room listens to a price signal from the harbour authority, and when the number drops below four cents the ovens, the dough proofers and the big walk-in freezer all wake up together.

The bakery is one of 312 businesses and a little over two thousand households that now buy what Port Calder calls night power: electricity generated by six turbines bolted to the seabed where the car ferry used to turn. The ferry stopped running in 2019. The dock it left behind had deep water, a grid connection sized for charging buses, and a harbour master with a spreadsheet.

"Everybody told us tidal was a science project," says Marit Solberg, who has run the harbour for fourteen years. "It is. But it is a science project that shows up twice a day whether or not the wind does."

A timetable instead of a forecast

The appeal of tidal generation has always been its predictability. Wind and solar output are forecast; tides are published, years ahead, to the minute. What has held the technology back is cost. Machines that live in salt water are expensive to build and worse to repair, and the sites with strong enough currents tend to be far from anyone who wants the power.

Port Calder had the opposite problem. The current was mediocre, about 2.4 metres per second at peak, but the customers were four hundred metres away. The town's bet was that a cheap, modest machine next to a willing buyer beats an excellent machine at the end of a long cable.

The turbines are small, 250 kilowatts each, and deliberately boring. They share a gearbox design with a line of river turbines that has been in production for a decade. When one fails, a harbour workboat lifts it out at slack water and drops in a spare; the swap takes under three hours and has happened nine times.

We stopped asking how to make the tide fit the town and started asking what the town could do at two in the morning.

Marit Solberg, harbour master

That question turned out to matter more than the hardware. Tides do not care about dinner time. Two of the four daily peaks land at night, and they drift by about fifty minutes every day. Selling that power into the regional market meant accepting whatever the market paid at three in the morning, which was often close to nothing.

Selling the unfashionable hours

So the harbour authority became a retailer. It offered a simple tariff to anyone inside the town boundary: a published price for every half hour of the coming month, lowest when the turbines would be running. Then it lent businesses the money to buy equipment that could wait: thermal stores, larger freezers, chargers for delivery vans.

The laundry that serves the coast's hotels moved its entire wash cycle to follow the tide table. The fish processor makes ice at night and sells the surplus to the boats. The primary school heats a tank the size of a shipping container and draws on it all day.

Not everyone was persuaded. The first winter, the price signal failed for two nights and a row of heat stores stayed cold. A café owner on the front, Dev Arora, remembers opening to a kitchen at nine degrees. "I told Marit I would go back to gas," he says. "She came down with a space heater and an engineer and stayed until lunch. Hard to stay angry at that."

The authority now publishes its outages the way it publishes the tides, on a board outside the harbour office and on the same web page as the tariff.

What the numbers leave out

Independent analysts are cautious about how far the model travels. Dr Lena Fischbach, who studies local energy markets, points out that Port Calder inherited its most expensive asset for free. "The grid connection and the dock were paid for by a ferry company that no longer exists," she says. "Take those away and the price per kilowatt-hour roughly doubles."

Even doubled, it would undercut the daytime rate. And the harbour's accounts show something the engineers did not predict: the turbines have become the smaller part of the business. Interest on equipment loans, and a share of the savings each customer makes, now bring in more than the electricity.

Three other harbours on the coast have sent delegations. Two have currents as good as Calder's. Only one, Solberg says, asked the right first question, which was not about turbines at all. They wanted to know how many of their neighbours owned a freezer.

Back on Quay Street the ovens finish at half past five, just as the tide turns. By the time the first customers arrive the bread is cooling and the price has climbed back to eleven cents. The baker, who asked not to be named because she does not want to explain the relay to anyone else, says she has not set an alarm in two years.
